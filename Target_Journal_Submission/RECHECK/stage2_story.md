# Stage 2 - problem story (2026-11-23)
Person and setting: a county or state environmental-health programme officer who can fund tests for only part of the private wells in the area. Burden: about 40 million people in the United States use private wells outside public-supply regulation (Amery et al. 2025, abstract read via PubMed). Owners are responsible for testing (MacDonald and Tippett 2020, abstract read). Documented practice: agencies target outreach by town rankings (Vermont/CDC 2026, PDF read), neighbour-exceedance letters (MacDonald and Tippett 2020), and offers to owners with earlier results (O'Neill et al. 2022). Documented failure of current tools: the national arsenic model reported 33.9% test sensitivity at 10 µg/L with a stratified random 30% test set and no spatial hold-out (Lombard et al. 2021, PMC full text read); similar maps are scored with random splits.
Cost statements: the paper's cost unit is TESTS PER UNSAFE WELL FOUND in a simulation. It is not a measured money cost; no price per test was used and no field cost was observed. The paper says so (Limits). The only external cost figures cited are the 40 million users and the 33.9% sensitivity, both verified from the cited sources.
Changes in the field since the first run (search 2026-11-23): lead-pipe programmes pair ML predictions with field verification (ActiveRemediation 2018; New York audit 2026); adaptive cluster sampling (Thompson 1990) is the classical form of 'follow the hits'; Cao et al. 2024 and Usman et al. 2026 report weak transfer of fluoride models. None changes the data, but all reduce the novelty of the qualitative message.
One-paragraph story: County health programme officers can test only some private wells for arsenic, uranium or fluoride, and every test that finds a safe well is a lost test, because national risk maps are judged with random splits and may not work in a state that supplied no data. We simulated testing campaigns on 53,294 arsenic, 28,117 uranium and 150,299 fluoride wells, holding out one state at a time, and proposed ABF, a rule that learns during the campaign how far to trust the map. In the simulation, testing next to wells that just came back unsafe cut tests per unsafe arsenic well from 9.2 to 5.2, while a map from other states was close to random order; for fluoride the map did transfer. No field trial was done.
First-page check: title, abstract and first paragraph state the problem, gap, evidence and limits in plain English (abstract 173 words, Flesch reading ease 63; introduction 44.8). The first page now also states that feedback itself is not new (close calls). Remaining issue: the title says adaptive testing 'outperforms' maps, which is supported for arsenic (all tests) and fluoride/arsenic>5 (ABF), not for uranium.
